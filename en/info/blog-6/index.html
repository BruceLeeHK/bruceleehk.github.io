How to Tell Termites and Ants Apart?

Termites are tiny — about the same size as ants — so the two are constantly mistaken for each other. Because of their secretive, hidden habits, termites are actually very hard to spot, especially for those of us without professional training. In fact, before you ever see the termites themselves, you are more likely to notice the signs they leave behind.

1. What Are Termites?

Termites (also called "white ants") live in the natural environment as primitive wood-boring insects. They have existed on Earth for more than 250 million years, and over 2,500 species are known worldwide, usually found in low-altitude primary forest regions of the subtropics. Termites are social insects whose behaviour resembles that of ants and bees. They live together in colonies under a strict caste system: a king, a queen, alates (reproductive swarmers), soldiers and workers. Each caste has its own specific function, and they cooperate to keep the colony running smoothly. By helping to break down dead trees and plant matter, they are even beneficial to the ecosystem. Of course, when termites invade human living spaces, they become a huge threat.

2. Termites Commonly Found in Hong Kong

In Hong Kong, the warm and humid climate is highly favourable for termite breeding, so the losses termites cause far exceed those from fire and flooding. They can eat wood non-stop, 24 hours a day. Termite damage leads to expensive repairs and can quickly destroy building structures, so to avoid lingering trouble, engage a pest control company as soon as possible for professional termite control and treatment.

The termite most commonly found in Hong Kong and Taiwan is the Formosan subterranean termite (Coptotermes formosanus), also known as the house termite or Taiwan house termite. Native to eastern China, Taiwan (from which this insect takes its name) and Japan, it is so destructive that it is nicknamed the "super termite" — this species builds huge colonies and devours wood at remarkable speed.

A single Formosan subterranean termite colony can contain several million individuals, whereas other species typically number only a few hundred thousand. Most termites love moisture and fear light: they live in colonies underground or inside tree trunks, and whenever they move or forage they stay beneath mud tubes (shelter tubes) built by the workers. Colony size varies by species — from just a few hundred to well over a million.

3. How Do Termites Get into Your Home?

Termites invade your home mainly through two routes. First, the reproductive alates in a colony — the future "kings" and "queens" — fly in search of a suitable habitat (including damp soil and earth); once they find one, they shed their wings and start laying eggs to establish a new colony. The other common entry route is through cracks at ground level in the building. Normally termites hide in the soil foraging for food: along mud tubes (shelter tubes) made of soil, wood fragments and their own secretions, they reach timber above ground and then work their way into your home.

4. What Is the Difference Between Termites and Ants?

Like other insects, termites have six legs and a body divided into three main parts: head, thorax and abdomen, with a pair of straight antennae on the head. People constantly confuse termites with ants mainly because their appearance is somewhat similar. The most obvious difference is the waist: an ant's waist is extremely slender, with the thorax and abdomen clearly separated, while a termite's thorax and abdomen blend together with no obvious division. Also, a termite's front and back wings are almost equal in size, while an ordinary ant's front and back wings differ in size; finally, a termite's antennae are straight, while an ordinary ant's are bent.

5. What Does a Termite Look Like?

Like other insects, termites have six legs and a body divided into three main parts: head, thorax and abdomen, with a pair of straight antennae on the head. People constantly confuse termites with ants mainly because their appearance is somewhat similar. Here is a side-by-side comparison of termites and ants:

  • Waist: a termite's waist is straight, while an ant's is extremely slender.
  • Antennae: a termite's antennae are straight, while an ant's are bent.
  • Wing length: a termite's four wings are all the same length, while an ant's front and back wings differ in length.
  • Discarded wings: flying ants do not shed their wings. If you see these flying insects at home you can be fairly confident; and if you also find discarded wings (usually on windowsills), you can be certain they are termites.
  • Body shape: a termite's thorax and abdomen blend together, giving a cylinder-shaped body; an ant has a clearly pinched "waist" between thorax and abdomen, giving an hourglass figure.

6. Signs of a Termite Infestation

Termites are very hard to spot directly, but they leave plenty of clues behind. If you notice any of the following signs at home, stay alert and engage a professional pest control company for an inspection as soon as possible:

  • Mud tubes on walls: to avoid being detected by humans while moving food, subterranean termites build tunnels out of mud, soil and debris. These tubes are about as wide as a coin, usually appear on the interior and exterior walls of the house, and link up to its entry points.
  • Swarmers (flying termites) or discarded wings: the first sign homeowners usually notice is a swarm of flying termites on the wing. Another telltale sign is termite wings left behind on windowsills or the floor. Termites shed their wings immediately after finding a mate, so these obvious discarded wings are solid evidence of a colony indoors.
  • Wood that sounds papery or hollow when tapped: termites eat wood from the inside out, leaving only a thin layer of timber or paint on the surface. So if you tap a wooden item that is infested, it will sound hollow or thin, because part (or all) of the wood inside has already been eaten away.
  • Tiny holes in walls: termites chew through timber and wallpaper fibres. Over time, pinholes or cracks may start to appear on the wall. If you see this and cannot find any explanation, take a closer look — it is often a sign of termite attack. Tap the wall and listen for a hollow sound; if you hear one, call a termite treatment company promptly, especially if the affected wall is a load-bearing wall (one that supports the floor or the roof).
  • Galleries inside wood: also known as feeding galleries, these are very difficult to detect from the outside.
  • Termite droppings: after consuming wood, drywood termites usually pass small brown pellet-like droppings, which tend to pile up beneath the infested timber.
