📲 The WhatsApp rescue call: running sounds above the ceiling every night

The owner's first WhatsApp message was impressively specific: a renovation worker had checked around the signboard — no bright lights attracting rats — and found one hole already sealed with steel wool. But the owner was certain: "It must be up in the false ceiling — there are running sounds every night" and "in the morning I've seen it run back up from the ground". Same appearance, same habits, same feeding pattern for months.

Our technician replied with one key fact straight away: a black rat can squeeze its whole body through a hole the size of a small cookie (about 2cm). Rather than guessing the entry point over chat, the sensible move is a professional on-site inspection. One message — "shall we locate it precisely on site?🙂" — and the visit was booked.

🔍 On-site inspection: three pieces of evidence in the false ceiling

Invited by the owner, our rodent technician arrived with torch and inspection tools and checked the shop inside out. The false ceiling told the story immediately — the cooker-hood duct was unsealed; large amounts of rat droppings lay along the cable trunks; and the shop's own glue boards already held 3–4 rat carcasses, big and small. From the droppings and the activity range, the technician confirmed a black rat (roof rat) infestation: these rodents favour high zones — ceilings, rooftops — and only come down at night to feed, especially on fresh dough. That matches the owner's camera footage of a shadow climbing onto the dough perfectly.

The inspection also exposed two entry risks: ① the cable-trunk runs were blocked from view, so some ceiling openings might not be fully sealed; the owner said rats came out of the floor drain — he covered it with a thick wooden board at night, but the board had been gnawed, with visible claw marks: proof it was already their daily runway; ② the toilet and nearby pipe penetrations were also potential entries — sewer rats are excellent swimmers. The owner additionally reported cockroaches near the toilet.

🧠 Why no rat poison? The "physical-first" rule for food shops

Almost every shop owner asks the same first question: "Why not just put down rat poison — isn't that faster?" For ordinary homes or back alleys, rodenticide is a common tool. Inside a running food shop, it is a different story — bait can contaminate ingredients, tableware or work surfaces; and rats poisoned with anticoagulant bait often die inside false ceilings or cavity walls, rotting and breeding flies — a second disaster right above your kitchen.

So this restaurant rodent control job used zero rodenticide: glue-board trapping with food bait, backed by source-proofing advice — the approach that matches food safety and HACCP restaurant compliance.

🪤 Setting the trap line: bait them with their favourite dough

Because black rats favour high ground — ceilings, rooftops — the main trap line went up into the false ceiling: multiple glue boards placed along cable-trunk openings and activity zones, each baited with ingredients straight from the shop's own kitchen: dough + sesame oil + special sauce + roasted peanuts. Baiting with food the rats already eat is the most reliable way to beat their fear of new objects; offer something unfamiliar and they often ignore it completely.

Besides the main ceiling line, the technician added two support positions: a rat bait tunnel on top of the cooker hood (for rats moving along the duct) and glue boards under the work counters (for the night-time descent to the floor) — high and low, leaving no runway uncovered.

🎯 The result: rats caught + a FREE cockroach treatment on the spot

The ceiling glue boards soon paid off — rats were caught, and the technician removed the carcasses promptly so nothing would rot above the kitchen. Better still: while re-checking, he noticed the cockroach issue the owner had mentioned was beginning to spread, so he threw in a FREE cockroach treatment on the spot — not a single extra dollar — dealing with both pests in one visit.

For food businesses, pest prevention is never "either rats or roaches" — they usually arrive together, following the same things: food scraps and water. A professional commercial pest control plan should therefore handle food-shop pest and rodent prevention as one package: rats and roaches together, cutting the problem at the root.

🛡️ Source-proofing advice: the 6mm rule in four steps

Catching rats stops the bleeding; sealing entry points ends the infestation. Guided by IPM (Integrated Pest Management) and combining AI smart diagnosis, physical control and professional treatments, our technician made four proofing recommendations for this site:

① Seal cable-trunk gaps and any ceiling openings larger than 6mm: a newborn rat can already pass through a 6mm gap; plastic sheets, foam and ordinary mesh will not stop rats — pack steel wool and finish with cement or a metal plate.

② Cement the pipe gaps + cover floor drains at night: seal the gaps around toilet and nearby pipes with cement; cover the floor drain with a thick, hard board or floor tile every night after closing — the gnawed board proved that drain was already their daily runway.

③ Switch to auto-closing exhaust fans: the fan opening is a shortcut for black rats from the external wall into the false ceiling; and when the toilet is not in use — or before closing at night — cover it with a hard board.

④ Housekeeping to cut the food chain: clean thoroughly and keep the shop tidy, use lidded bins, and disinfect with diluted bleach to remove lingering rodent odours — if the smell stays, their kin will still think food is available; store ingredients in stainless-steel cabinets to cut off the food source completely.

🍜 Food-shop pest & rodent prevention: the owner's nightly checklist

We distilled the technician's recommendations into a one-page checklist for anyone in the food business:

  • First thing at closing: all ingredients, dough and fillings into stainless-steel cabinets or fridges — never left on worktops overnight;
  • Bin: lidded bin, emptied the same day, nothing left overnight;
  • Floor drain: cover with a thick board or floor tile before closing;
  • Exhaust fan: switch to an auto-closing model; cover the toilet when not in use;
  • Weekly check: false ceiling, cable trunks, corners — look for fresh droppings, gnaw or claw marks; photograph anything you find;
  • On any sign: WhatsApp photos to our technician for remote assessment immediately — never delay: one pair of rats can multiply into over a thousand descendants in a year.

Food-safety hygiene is the lifeline of any restaurant. The FEHD takes rodent signs in food premises extremely seriously — a bust means prosecution and may even affect licence renewal. Instead of waiting to be caught, book routine commercial pest control and stay ahead.

Commercial Pest Control & Restaurant Rodent Control FAQ

Can restaurants and snack shops use rat poison?

Not recommended. In food environments bait can contaminate ingredients or tableware; worse, rats poisoned with anticoagulant bait often die inside false ceilings or cavity walls, then rot and breed flies — a second disaster for a food business. The professional approach is physical trapping (glue boards + food bait) combined with source proofing, in line with food safety and HACCP principles. This Sha Kei Wan case used zero rodenticide — and still got the job done.

How do you tell a black rat from a sewer rat?

Black rats (roof rats) are slim, with tails longer than the body, big ears and protruding eyes. They love climbing and stay in false ceilings, cable trunks and rooftops, coming down at night to feed. Sewer rats (Norway rats) are bulky and live in drains, basements and ground level — and they swim well. In this case, droppings, activity height and the "night-time running sounds above the ceiling" all pointed to black rats, so the main trap line went up into the false ceiling.

Where should glue boards be placed for best results?

Three spots: ① along the rats’ current runway (cable-trunk openings, next to floor drains), tight to the wall; ② at the centre of activity inside the false ceiling; ③ with bait the rats already eat (here: dough + sesame oil + special sauce + roasted peanuts) to beat neophobia. Rats avoid flat, exposed glue boards — folding them into a 3D "bait box" works far better; see our glue-board folding tutorial.
