Compared with the Bible's emphasis on the 'Creator' and the 'created', how are they similar or different? And why did Jesus refer to moths destroying treasures when teaching about wealth?

Introduction: Facing a Pest Problem, What Can We Learn from Faith Beyond Frustration?

Many Hong Kong families, and even church premises, have faced the trouble of termites gnawing at wooden crosses or bed bugs hiding in the crevices of wooden chairs. As we worry about how to get rid of pests, it is worth pausing to think: what role do insects play in the eyes of the Creator? Taoism advocates 'the unity of heaven and humanity', reminding people to live in harmony with nature; how does the Bible view the relationship between the 'Creator', 'people' and 'insects'?

Some ask: 'Which came first, the chicken or the egg? The ant egg or the termite?' Such questions sound like jokes, yet they strike at the core — where do we come from, and why does anything exist? Theories such as the Big Bang and evolution remain hotly debated, with doubts only piling up; the Bible, however, opens with a clear statement: 'In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.' (Genesis 1:1) God also told Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' (Exodus 3:14) — apart from God, everything is created, insects included. Only by recognising this order can we understand the spiritual meaning behind pest problems.

Revelation 1: Did God Create Insects Before Humans to Teach Us Humility?

Scripture records that on the sixth day God first created the living creatures of the earth — 'Let the land produce living creatures according to their kinds: the livestock, the creatures that move along the ground, and the wild animals, according to their kinds' — and only then made mankind 'in our image, in our likeness', blessing them: 'Be fruitful and increase in number; fill the earth and subdue it. Rule over the fish in the sea and the birds in the sky and over every living creature that moves on the ground.' (Genesis 1:24-28) From this passage we learn that insects and humans were both created on the sixth day, and that God gave humanity authority and stewardship over insects.

Ancient Jewish rabbis offered a humorous yet profound interpretation of this: God created insects before humans in order to keep people humble. When someone becomes arrogant, God can remind them: 'Even a tiny mosquito was created before you — what grounds do you have for pride?' On the other hand, when people look down on themselves, they can notice that God placed humanity last in the order of creation, as if the entire procession of creation was paving the way for our arrival.

What matters more is this: God created insects, yet He did not keep the management for Himself — He entrusted the authority to 'subdue the earth' to humanity. This was part of God's original purpose in creating people, and it was an act of trust. We are responsible for wisely 'ruling over' these creatures rather than letting pest infestations destroy our lives. John 1:18 says no one has ever seen God, yet through everything He has made, we can recognise His wisdom and order everywhere.

Revelation 2: Are Pest Infestations Natural, or God's Discipline?

From the ten plagues in the Book of Exodus, we see God using the plague of frogs, gnats, flies and locusts to discipline the Pharaoh who blocked the Israelites from going to the wilderness to worship Him — four of the ten plagues involved insects or swarms. In the New Testament's Acts, King Herod, in his arrogance, refused to give glory to God and was ultimately 'eaten by worms and died' (Acts 12:23). Clearly, tiny insects have, at specific moments in history, served as instruments the Creator used to awaken and discipline people.

The Bible also says God saw all that He had made, and it was 'very good' (Genesis 1:31). The suffering caused by pests entered the world in connection with the curse on the ground after Adam's sin, affecting his descendants. Whether or not one is a believer, the old saying 'Man proposes, God disposes' reminds us: however much wealth or power we hold, we should live and work with reverence.

Revelation 3: Peter's Vision — Breaking the Frame of 'Unclean'

In Leviticus, God gave the Israelites strict dietary rules: all flying insects that walk on all fours are to be regarded as detestable, except those that have jointed legs for hopping on the ground — locusts, crickets and grasshoppers of various kinds may be eaten (Leviticus 11:20-23). Peter once said, 'Nothing impure or unclean has ever entered my mouth.'

Yet in Acts 11, through a vision that included 'insects', the Lord three times commanded Peter: 'Do not call anything impure that God has made clean.' This was not merely about dietary law — the deeper meaning was God's revelation that Peter should break through his prejudice and bring the gospel to the Gentiles. If even the frame of 'unclean' could be broken by the Lord Himself, then the relationship between people and created things has always been defined by God.

Conclusion: Store Up Treasures in Heaven, Manage Earthly Possessions Wisely

Jesus taught: 'Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moths and vermin destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moths and vermin do not destroy, and where thieves do not break in and steal.' (Matthew 6:19-20) These words remain strikingly real in today's Hong Kong — properties people have worked hard to buy, and precious wooden furniture, can easily be ruined by termites or wood borers if left unmanaged.

While we store up treasures in heaven, we should also fulfil our duty as 'good stewards' of the possessions God has entrusted to us on earth — including our homes and church buildings. Getting 'pest control' done is not merely a household chore; it can be a spiritual discipline of carrying out our stewardship.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

The Bible says God created insects before humans — can Christians still exterminate pests?

Absolutely. After creating insects on the sixth day, God made humans in His own image and blessed them: 'Rule over the earth, and manage every living creature on the ground' (Genesis 1:28). Since insects were placed under human 'stewardship', controlling an infestation that threatens family health or damages home and church property is precisely carrying out that stewardship — the Bible never asks people to live side by side with pests. What matters is doing it with reverence for the Creator, not abusing what He has made.

Why would God use such tiny insects to discipline Pharaoh and King Herod?

Four of the ten plagues in Exodus involved insects (frogs, gnats, flies, locusts), and Acts 12:23 records that Herod was 'eaten by worms and died'. God choosing the smallest creatures to carry out discipline shows that glory and power do not depend on human size but on God's will. Even mosquitoes obey the Creator — how much more should people learn humility.

How does the Taoist 'unity of heaven and humanity' differ from the biblical 'subdue the earth'?

Both value harmony between people and nature, but they start from different points: the unity of heaven and humanity sees people as part of nature who should follow nature; the Bible teaches that people are made in God's image and entrusted with stewardship — the earth belongs to God and we are merely trustees. So when Christians face pest problems, the answer is neither exterminating nature out of hostility nor passively doing nothing, but managing earthly possessions with wisdom and reverence for the Creator.

In summary: bugs came first, humans came after — God teaches us humility through the order of creation, awakens people through tiny insects, and entrusts stewardship to humanity.
